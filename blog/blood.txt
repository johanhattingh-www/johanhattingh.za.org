Date: October 05, 2026
Subject: The Blank Badge

The letter on my donor card says O positive. I've carried it for twenty years and never asked what the letter physically means on the surface of each cell.

Chromosome 9. One gene. One missing nucleotide at position 261. The enzyme that should stamp a sugar onto the H antigen misfolds and quits. Every red blood cell I own — 25 trillion of them right now — carries a blank badge where type A has N-acetylgalactosamine and type B has D-galactose. The blank badge is not damage. It's the original design. The stamps came later.

That bare surface is why my blood enters almost anyone. The immune system scans for shapes. Anti-A antibodies hunt N-acetylgalactosamine. Anti-B hunt D-galactose. They find my H antigen and move on. Nothing to grab. Universal donor because there's nothing to attack.

The same bare surface that hides from malaria parasites — they anchor poorly on type O cells, smaller rosettes, less severe disease — also leaves me vulnerable. Helicobacter pylori binds the exposed H antigen in the stomach lining. Vibrio cholerae toxin grabs it in the gut. Malaria pressure pushed O frequency above 60% in parts of Africa. Cholera pulls it back.

My von Willebrand factor runs lower because the A and B antigens aren't there to protect it from clearance. Five to twenty percent less clotting risk. One deletion. Three consequences pulling different directions. The 37% global frequency is where the trade-off settled.

Wian asked me last week why my blood is "special." I told him it's not special. It's just honest about what's missing.
