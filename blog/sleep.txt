Date: September 27, 2026
Subject: Sleep Inertia and the Ninth Hour

Liesl caught me staring into a cold mug on the back step on Saturday. I had slept nine hours, no alarm, and woke up feeling like someone had replaced my kneecaps with dry gravel. On five hours I can teach three double periods of electrostatics and referee under-eleven rugby without noticing my shins. On nine, my boots felt heavy before I even tied the laces.

Most folks assume sleep works like filling an empty petrol tank: more litres poured in should simply mean more distance down the road. Biology does not care for that picture. Sleep cycles run in ninety-minute waves, climbing from light drifting down into deep slow-wave repair and back up again. When you sleep seven hours, you generally surface near the top of a wave. Stretch it out to nine, and the odds shift. You rip yourself out from the dark, heavy trough at the bottom.

Clinicians call that leaden fog sleep inertia. It is not exhaustion. It is a phase error. Your brain is trying to strike a match while the chemical taps for waking are still half-closed.

Liesl did not look up from her tea. "You didn't need rest," she said. "You were just hiding."
